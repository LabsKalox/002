$32,995. That's where the new 2027 Subaru Outback Base starts. Subaru says that lowers the Outback's entry price by $2,000. But that compares the new Base with last year's Premium, a different trim. The 2027 Premium stays at $34,995.

So, is the Base enough, or is the Premium worth another $2,000?

Short answer. If the lowest price matters most, the Base covers the essentials. If you'll use a power driver's seat, rear-seat vents, rear USB ports, and a spare tire, the Premium makes sense. Let's see where that $2,000 goes.

Start with the Base, because this is not a stripped-out model.

Every 2027 Outback Base gets Symmetrical All-Wheel Drive. It uses the 2.5-liter BOXER engine with 180 horsepower, paired with a Lineartronic CVT. You also get X-MODE and Hill Descent Control, plus 8.7 inches of ground clearance. So the Base keeps the hardware many people buy an Outback for in the first place.

The tech isn't cut down either. The Base has the 12.1-inch touchscreen, a 12.3-inch digital instrument cluster, and wireless Apple CarPlay and Android Auto. Those are not upgrade-only features here.

For driver assistance, the Base includes EyeSight Driver Assist and DriverFocus.

For cold mornings, it has heated front seats, heated side mirrors, and a windshield wiper de-icer. Add a power rear gate and dual-zone automatic climate control, and the Base covers most of the daily comfort basics.

The visible giveaways are the 18-inch silver-finish alloy wheels and the Black Tricot cloth seats.

So the Base isn't missing the big things. The question is whether the smaller things are worth $2,000 to you.

The Premium starts at $34,995. That's exactly $2,000 more than the Base. Here's what Subaru adds.

First, the driver's seat. The Premium gets a 10-way power-adjustable driver's seat with 2-way power lumbar support. If you share the car with another driver, or you spend long days behind the wheel, this is probably the upgrade you'll notice most. If you're the only driver and you set your seat once, it matters a lot less.

Second, the back seat. The Premium adds rear-seat climate control vents and illuminated rear USB-A and USB-C ports. For a family with kids, or anyone who regularly carries adults in back, these are everyday features. If your rear seat mostly carries groceries, you'll barely use them.

Third, the upholstery. The Base uses cloth. The Premium uses StarTex, which Subaru markets as water-repellent. If you deal with kids, pets, or wet gear, that can matter. If you simply prefer cloth, the Base gives you that.

Fourth, the spare tire. The Premium includes a temporary space-saver spare. The Base doesn't list one. If you cover long rural stretches or roads where help is far away, that can be the strongest single argument for the Premium. If you mostly drive in town, it's less critical, but check what the Base comes with instead.

The rest is smaller. A leather-wrapped steering wheel. A multi-use cargo cover to hide what's in the back. Vibrating steering-wheel alerts. And 18-inch dark gray alloy wheels instead of silver.

Put it together and the pattern is clear. The Premium doesn't add capability, a bigger screen, or more power. It adds comfort, convenience, and a spare tire.

Now the numbers, because this is where buyers get confused.

Both 2027 trims carry the same standard destination charge of $1,545. So the Base comes to $34,540, and the Premium comes to $36,540. That's before taxes, options, and other fees. The gap stays at exactly $2,000.

Now compare the Premium year over year. The 2026 Premium was $34,995. The 2027 Premium is $34,995. Same MSRP. But destination went from $1,450 to $1,545. So once destination is included, a 2027 Premium actually costs $95 more than a 2026 Premium.

The lower 2027 entry price is real. But it comes from adding a new, lower trim. It is not a price cut on the same car.

One more caveat. Subaru lists a different destination charge for Alaska, and it may vary in some states. So treat these totals as standard figures, not a national out-the-door price.

Next, what is not standard.

The Premium does not come standard with a moonroof or navigation. Those are available through an option package that Subaru lists at $2,045 MSRP, depending on configuration.

That changes the math. A Premium with that package comes to $37,040 MSRP, or $38,585 with standard destination. That's $4,045 more than a Base at $34,540.

So if you see a Premium on a lot with a moonroof, that car has the package. The moonroof is not part of the $2,000 step. Read the sticker before you compare prices.

Here's how I'd decide, by buyer type.

If you're a budget-focused buyer, get the Base. You keep all-wheel drive, the same engine, the big screens, EyeSight, heated front seats, and the power rear gate. The $2,000 stays in your pocket.

If you're a family with regular back-seat passengers, lean Premium. Rear vents and rear USB ports get used on every trip, not once a year.

If seat adjustment matters, because you share the car or you're picky about lumbar support, lean Premium. The power seat is the most personal upgrade on the list.

If a spare tire matters, because you drive long distances or rural roads, lean Premium. That one item can justify the step by itself.

And if you want a moonroof or navigation, budget for the Premium plus the $2,045 package, not just the Premium.

Before you sign, run this quick checklist.

One. Get the window sticker for both trims, and confirm both say 2027.

Two. Check the destination line. Standard is $1,545, but Alaska is different, and some states may vary.

Three. Compare equipment line by line, especially the power seat, rear vents, rear USB ports, and spare tire.

Four. Check whether an option package or dealer-added accessories are raising the price.

Five. Compare final written prices, including taxes and fees, not just MSRP.

And keep timing in mind. Subaru says the 2027 Outback arrives at retailers later this year, so availability can differ by dealer and region.

Here's my verdict. Start with the Base. It's the value pick, and it isn't missing the core hardware. Move up to the Premium only if at least two of these four matter to you: the power driver's seat, the rear-seat vents, the rear USB ports, and the spare tire. If they do, the $2,000 is easy to justify. If they don't, you'd be paying for features you won't use.

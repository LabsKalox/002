$32,995. That's where the new 2027 Subaru Outback Base starts. Subaru says that lowers the Outback's entry price by $2,000. But that compares the new Base with last year's Premium, a different trim. The 2027 Premium stays at $34,995.

So, is the Base enough, or is the Premium worth another $2,000?

Short answer. If the lowest price matters most, the Base covers the essentials. If you'll use a power driver's seat, rear-seat vents, rear USB ports, and a spare tire, the Premium makes sense. Let's see where that $2,000 goes.

Start with the Base, because this is not a stripped-out model.

Every 2027 Outback Base gets Symmetrical All-Wheel Drive. It uses the 2.5-liter BOXER engine with 180 horsepower, paired with a Lineartronic CVT. And here's the key point. The Premium uses that same engine, that same transmission, and that same all-wheel-drive system. So the $2,000 does not buy you more power.

You also get X-MODE and Hill Descent Control, plus 8.7 inches of ground clearance. If your life includes snowy driveways, gravel roads, or steep trailhead parking, that's the hardware built for it, and the Base already has all of it.

The tech isn't cut down either. The Base has the 12.1-inch touchscreen, a 12.3-inch digital instrument cluster, and wireless Apple CarPlay and Android Auto. That last part matters more than it sounds. Because the Base connects to your phone wirelessly, you can run the map and music apps you already use on that big screen. Keep that in mind when we get to the option package.

For driver assistance, the Base includes EyeSight Driver Assist and DriverFocus. EyeSight is Subaru's camera-based driver-assist suite, and DriverFocus is its driver-attention monitoring system. Both are standard, so core safety tech is not what separates these two trims.

For cold mornings, the Base has heated front seats, heated side mirrors, and a windshield wiper de-icer. That's the cold-weather kit many buyers in snow states look for first. Add a power rear gate and dual-zone automatic climate control, so the driver and front passenger can each set their own temperature, and the Base covers most of the daily comfort basics.

The visible giveaways are the 18-inch silver-finish alloy wheels and the Black Tricot cloth seats.

So the Base isn't missing the big things. The question is whether the smaller things are worth $2,000 to you.

The Premium starts at $34,995. That's exactly $2,000 more than the Base. Here's what Subaru adds.

First, the driver's seat. The Premium gets a 10-way power-adjustable driver's seat with 2-way power lumbar support. If you share the car with another driver, or you spend long days behind the wheel, this is probably the upgrade you'll notice most. Power adjustment also makes it easier to fine-tune your position on a long trip, and the power lumbar helps you dial in lower-back support. If you're the only driver and you set your seat once, it matters a lot less.

Second, the back seat. The Premium adds rear-seat climate control vents and illuminated rear USB-A and USB-C ports. For a family with kids, or anyone who regularly carries adults in back, these are everyday features. Tablets, phones, and hot summer afternoons all land in the back seat. If your rear seat mostly carries groceries, you'll barely use them.

Third, the upholstery. The Base uses cloth. The Premium uses StarTex, which Subaru markets as water-repellent. If you deal with kids, pets, or wet gear, that can matter. If you simply prefer cloth, the Base gives you that.

Fourth, the spare tire. The Premium includes a temporary space-saver spare. The Base doesn't list one. If you cover long rural stretches or roads where help is far away, that can be the strongest single argument for the Premium. If you mostly drive in town, it's less critical, but check what the Base comes with instead.

The rest is smaller. A leather-wrapped steering wheel, which you touch on every drive, but which doesn't change what the car can do. A multi-use cargo cover, which keeps gear out of sight when you park at a trailhead or in a city lot. Vibrating steering-wheel alerts, which give you a physical cue through the wheel. And 18-inch dark gray alloy wheels instead of silver.

Put it together and the pattern is clear. The Premium doesn't add capability, a bigger screen, or more power. It adds comfort, convenience, and a spare tire.

Here's a simple way to judge whether that's worth it. Take the $2,000 and spread it over five years of ownership. That's $400 a year, or about $33 a month.

So the real question becomes this. Would you pay about $33 a month for a power seat, rear vents, rear USB ports, StarTex seats, and a spare tire? For a family that uses the back seat every day, that can be an easy yes. For a solo commuter, it's probably money better kept.

That's simple cash math. It doesn't include sales tax, financing, or any resale effect, and we're not estimating those here.

Now the numbers, because this is where buyers get confused.

Both 2027 trims carry the same standard destination charge of $1,545. So the Base comes to $34,540, and the Premium comes to $36,540. That's before taxes, options, and other fees. The gap stays at exactly $2,000.

And if your state charges sales tax on the vehicle price, that tax applies to the extra $2,000 too. So your real gap can end up a little larger than $2,000.

Now compare the Premium year over year. The 2026 Premium was $34,995. The 2027 Premium is $34,995. Same MSRP. But destination went from $1,450 to $1,545. So once destination is included, a 2027 Premium actually costs $95 more than a 2026 Premium.

If a dealer still has a 2026 Premium in stock, don't assume either one is the better deal. Put the two window stickers side by side and compare the written prices. The MSRP is the same, so the difference comes down to destination, dealer pricing, and the equipment listed on each sticker.

The lower 2027 entry price is real. But it comes from adding a new, lower trim. It is not a price cut on the same car.

One more caveat. Subaru lists a different destination charge for Alaska, and it may vary in some states. So treat these totals as standard figures, not a national out-the-door price.

Next, what is not standard.

The Premium does not come standard with a moonroof or navigation. Those are available through an option package that Subaru lists at $2,045 MSRP, depending on configuration.

That changes the math. A Premium with that package comes to $37,040 MSRP, or $38,585 with standard destination. That's $4,045 more than a Base at $34,540.

Remember what we said about the screen. Both trims already have wireless Apple CarPlay and Android Auto. If phone navigation works for you, built-in navigation alone may not be worth paying for. For many buyers, the moonroof is the bigger reason to look at that package.

So if you see a Premium on a lot with a moonroof, that car has the package. The moonroof is not part of the $2,000 step. Read the sticker before you compare prices.

Before we pick a winner, here are four mistakes I'd avoid.

Mistake one. Comparing MSRP on one car with a total price on another. $32,995 is MSRP. $34,540 includes destination. Always compare like with like, every single time.

Mistake two. Comparing a 2027 Base with a 2026 Premium and calling the difference a price cut. They're different trims with different equipment.

Mistake three. Assuming every Premium has a moonroof because the one on the lot does. Check the package line on the sticker.

Mistake four. Assuming the trim you want is sitting at your dealer today. Subaru says the 2027 Outback arrives at retailers later this year, so ask what your dealer can actually order and when, and get any delivery estimate in writing.

Here's how I'd decide, by buyer type.

If you're a budget-focused buyer, get the Base. You keep all-wheel drive, the same engine, the big screens, EyeSight, heated front seats, and the power rear gate. The $2,000 stays in your pocket.

If you're a family with regular back-seat passengers, lean Premium. Rear vents and rear USB ports get used on every trip, not once a year.

If seat adjustment matters, because you share the car or you're picky about lumbar support, lean Premium. The power seat is the most personal upgrade on the list.

If a spare tire matters, because you drive long distances or rural roads, lean Premium. That one item can justify the step by itself.

And if you want a moonroof or navigation, budget for the Premium plus the $2,045 package, not just the Premium.

Now let's make it simple with a two-out-of-four test. The four items are the power driver's seat, the rear-seat vents, the rear USB ports, and the spare tire. If at least two of them matter to you, the Premium earns its price.

Let's run it on a few typical buyers.

A solo commuter who drives mostly in town. No regular rear passengers, sets the seat once, rarely far from help. That's zero, maybe one out of four. Base.

A family of four with two kids in back. Rear vents and rear USB ports both count. That's two out of four already. Premium.

A couple sharing one car who take weekend trips on back roads. The power seat counts, and so does the spare tire. Two out of four. Premium.

A buyer who mainly wants a moonroof. That's not on the list at all. You're really shopping for a Premium with the package, so plan on about $4,045 over the Base, before taxes.

Before you sign, run this quick checklist.

One. Get the window sticker for both trims, and confirm both say 2027.

Two. Check the destination line. Standard is $1,545, but Alaska is different, and some states may vary.

Three. Compare equipment line by line, especially the power seat, rear vents, rear USB ports, and spare tire.

Four. Check whether an option package or dealer-added accessories are raising the price.

Five. If you're paying for the Premium, ask to see the spare tire in the car, and sit in both seats before you decide.

Six. Compare final written prices, including taxes and fees, not just MSRP.

Here's my verdict. Start with the Base. It's the value pick, and it isn't missing the core hardware. Move up to the Premium only if it passes the two-out-of-four test. If it does, the $2,000 is easy to justify. If it doesn't, you'd be paying for features you won't use.
